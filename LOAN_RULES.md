# How landlord and investor property loans work in the UK

A plain-English guide for Canary, the loan early-warning desk.

Researched on 12 November 2026 (Europe/London). Every market figure below is taken from a cited source. Worked-example numbers are clearly marked **ILLUSTRATIVE** and are not real loans, valuations or rents. Where a typical market number could not be verified, that is stated and no figure is invented.

This is a research note for a student hackathon project. It is not legal, credit or investment advice. Actual loan tests are defined in each facility agreement or mortgage conditions, and those definitions always win over a general guide.

---

## 1. The simple idea

A homeowner mortgage is repaid from a person's salary. A landlord or investor loan is repaid from the building's rent (and, if things go badly, from selling the building).

That is why the lender does not only ask "will this person pay?". It also writes rules into the contract about the building: is the rent still covering the interest, and is the loan still a safe share of the building's value? Those rules are called **covenants**. They are usually checked every quarter. Canary's job is to notice that a rule is heading for a break *between* those quarterly reports, using building-operations data.

## 2. How a landlord gets the loan

### 2.1 Buy-to-let (BTL) mortgages — one home or a small portfolio

These are the everyday landlord loans on a house, a flat, or several such properties. High-street banks, building societies and specialist BTL lenders provide them. They are usually secured by a first legal charge (a mortgage) over the property. Many are interest-only, meaning the landlord pays only interest during the term and repays the capital later, often by selling or refinancing the property (Barclays Intermediaries, loan terms).

**Underwriting is set by the Prudential Regulation Authority.** Supervisory Statement SS13/16 applies to PRA-regulated firms doing BTL lending that is not already covered by FCA consumer-mortgage rules. It does **not** apply to specialist corporate / commercial-banking investment or development finance (SS13/16, para 1.3(e)). The January 2026 update of SS13/16 is the text reviewed for this note (the Bank of England states that this update takes effect on 1 January 2027 to accompany Basel 3.1 final rules; the 125% industry-standard ICR, the 2-percentage-point / 5.5% stress, and the four-property portfolio definition have been in the statement since 2016).

What SS13/16 actually requires:

- Affordability must be assessed from rent and/or personal income. Firms must not rely on equity in the property or on a hoped-for rise in house prices (paras 2.1–2.2).
- The **interest coverage ratio (ICR)** is expected monthly rental income divided by monthly interest, after allowing for likely future rate rises (para 2.3).
- Expected rent should be checked by an independent valuer, an automated valuation, or an existing tenancy, with proper controls (para 2.4).
- When setting the minimum ICR, the lender should allow for costs the landlord pays: management and letting fees, council tax, service charge, insurance, repairs, voids, utilities, gas and electrical certificates, licence fees, ground rent and similar costs — for both individuals and companies (para 2.5). Tax, including the reduced deductibility of mortgage interest, should also be considered (para 2.6).
- **"The current industry standard is to set the minimum ICR threshold at 125%."** The PRA does not expect firms to go below that, and some of the cost and tax factors may justify a higher threshold (para 2.7).
- The interest-rate stress should look at least five years ahead, unless the rate is fixed or capped for five years or more (or for the whole term if shorter). Firms should have regard to market expectations, **a minimum increase of 2 percentage points**, and any Financial Policy Committee recommendation. Even if that stressed rate would be below **5.5%** in the first five years, firms should assume a minimum borrower rate of 5.5% (paras 2.11–2.14). Assumed rent growth used to offset rate rises must not exceed 2% (para 2.16).

**What lenders do in practice (examples, not a universal rule).** Many lenders still use 125% ICR for limited companies and basic-rate taxpayers, and 145% for higher-rate taxpayers, at a stressed rate. Published examples:

- Virgin Money: 125% ICR for basic-rate taxpayers and 145% for higher or additional-rate taxpayers; stress is the higher of 5.5% or pay rate + 2% on fixes shorter than five years, and the higher of 4.5% or pay rate + 1% on five-year-plus fixes (Virgin Money Intermediaries, rental cover).
- Accord / Yorkshire Building Society, as reported by the National Residential Landlords Association in December 2024: ICR remains 125% for basic-rate and 145% for higher-rate taxpayers, with specified stress-rate cuts (NRLA, December 2024).
- Metro Bank rental criteria (current published guide when fetched): 125% for limited companies and basic-rate individuals, 145% for higher-rate individuals; maximum LTV 80% for new customers; portfolio (4+ mortgaged BTLs) assessed at 140% (personal) or 125% (limited company) at 5.5%.
- The Mortgage Works: on a portfolio, aggregate ICR of 145% for personally owned property and 125% for limited-company property, at a 5.25% stress, with a 75% maximum LTV shown on the personally owned line of its portfolio table.

**LTV.** Loan-to-value is the loan divided by the property's value. Barclays' published BTL criteria cap standard BTL at **75% LTV** for loans up to £1.1 million (and 60% above that, up to £2 million), with tighter caps on some flats, new builds and high flood-risk property. The same page points to a specialist subsidiary for applicants who need more than 75%. The Mortgage Works advertises products available up to 80% LTV. So 75% is a common high-street maximum; 80% exists on some specialist products. It is not a legal cap.

**Portfolio landlords.** SS13/16 says a borrower with **four or more distinct mortgaged BTL properties**, counted in aggregate, should be treated as a portfolio landlord. The lender should use a specialist underwriting approach and look at experience, the full portfolio and mortgages, assets and liabilities (including tax), the business plan, and historical and expected cash flows across all properties (paras 3.1–3.4). Barclays, Accord and The Mortgage Works all use this four-or-more definition in their published criteria.

**Limited companies and personal guarantees.** Many landlords buy through a special-purpose limited company (an SPV). That does not automatically make the loan "non-recourse". The Mortgage Works' published limited-company criteria state: **"Personal guarantees are required from all directors in all cases,"** and applicants must take independent legal advice on the guarantee. Other lenders' rules vary; this note does not claim every BTL lender requires a guarantee.

**The process (Metro Bank's published BTL process is a clear high-street example):**

1. Agreement in Principle — outline rent, income and commitments, and an indication of how much may be lent.
2. Full application and supporting documents.
3. Independent valuation of the property (and a rental assessment).
4. Underwriting.
5. Formal mortgage offer, copied to the solicitor.
6. Legal searches, deposit, and then funds are released on completion.

### 2.2 Bigger residential blocks: build-to-rent (BTR) and student housing (PBSA)

A 200-flat rented block is not usually funded with a stack of high-street BTL mortgages. It is an **investment loan** (if the block is already built and let) or a **development loan** (if it is being built), provided by banks, insurers and debt funds. SS13/16 expressly carves this specialist investment and development finance out of the BTL mortgage statement.

Bayes Business School's Commercial Real Estate Lending Survey year-end 2025 — the long-running industry benchmark, with around 70 contributing lenders — shows that residential investment and student housing are now a meaningful part of the UK commercial real estate (CRE) debt market. In 2025, new senior loans were originated at an **average LTV of 58.6% on residential** and **57.3% on student housing**, with **average ICR of 1.2x (residential)** and **1.4x (student housing)**. These are origination averages across the survey, not the minimum covenant in any one contract.

A specialist BTR broker (Build to Rent Finance, reviewed June 2026) describes *indicative* terms for stabilised BTR investment debt as roughly **55–65% LTV**, terms of **5–10 years**, and a **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) typically around 1.3–1.5 times**, sized on **net operating income** (rent after voids, bad debt and operating costs). A PBSA broker (Student Accommodation Finance, reviewed June 2026) similarly describes senior loans on a stabilised student scheme as typically **55–65% LTV**, sized to the lower of LTV and what the net income will support on an interest-cover test, with occupancy, operator quality and income structure (direct-let, university nomination, or lease-backed) doing a lot of the work. These are broker indications, not a regulator or survey statistic. The exact ICR multiple for PBSA, that broker says, varies by lender and income security.

**Development vs investment.** A development loan pays for the build and is usually drawn in stages as a monitoring surveyor certifies progress. A practitioner guide (Barrow Street Advisors, UK Financing Guide) describes typical UK development parameters of about **55–65% of cost (LTC)** and a cap around **65–70% of gross development value (GDV)**. Once the block is built and let, the borrower often refinances onto cheaper long-term investment debt. Those Barrow Street ranges are practitioner ranges, not Bayes survey averages.

### 2.3 Commercial investment and development loans (office, retail, industrial)

Same family of loan as the block loans above, but the income usually comes from a small number of business leases rather than hundreds of residential tenancies.

**Who lends.** Bayes YE 2025 records £52.7 billion of new UK CRE loan origination in 2025 and £174.6 billion of outstanding loans, from UK banks and building societies, international banks, insurance companies and debt funds (alternative lenders held 45% of the market in the survey's 2025 fact sheet). The largest 12 originators that year were five UK banks, three international banks and four debt funds.

**Typical starting leverage and cover (Bayes YE 2025, average terms on loans originated in 2025):**

| Sector (survey label) | Average LTV | Average ICR |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Prime office | 56.8% | 1.6x |
| Prime London office | 56.7% | 1.4x |
| Regional city offices | 57.0% | 1.7x |
| Secondary office | 57.3% | 1.8x |
| Prime retail | 56.3% | 1.3x |
| Secondary retail | 54.2% | 1.5x |
| Prime logistics | 57.4% | 1.8x |
| Secondary logistics | 56.5% | 1.5x |
| Hotel | 56.9% | 1.3x |
| Student housing | 57.3% | 1.4x |
| Residential | 58.6% | 1.2x |

A chart caption in the same presentation says loan-to-value for new acquisition lending moved up in 2025 and that **"lenders are offering 60% LTV across property sectors."** That is a survey observation about origination, not a covenant floor or cap in a standard contract.

A practitioner guide (Barrow Street) places typical UK *senior* LTV in a **50–65%** band, and notes that this is more conservative than typical US senior leverage of **60–75%**. It also notes shorter UK investment-loan terms (often **three to seven years**) against five-to-ten-year terms more common in the US, and more relationship / recourse lending in the UK than in the largely non-recourse US institutional market. Clearing-bank, challenger, insurer and debt-fund ranges in that guide are practitioner ranges and should be treated as such. Insurers are described there as longer-term, often fixed-rate lenders (sometimes 10–25 years) who like stable income and long, secure leases.

**The commercial process**, from law-firm guides (Hamlins; EM Law):

1. **Application** and a pack: rent roll, leases, accounts, business plan, existing debt.
2. **Valuation** by an independent valuer acceptable to the lender.
3. **Underwriting** of the income, the tenant, the building and the borrower.
4. **Term sheet** (or heads of terms). Hamlins: this sets the commercial terms, including the financial covenants, and is generally not legally binding except for some clauses such as costs. Once signed, those terms are usually locked into the facility agreement.
5. **Facility agreement** and security documents (legal mortgage, often a debenture and a share charge; security over rent accounts and insurance). Syndicated deals may use the Loan Market Association (LMA) real estate finance form.
6. **Conditions precedent**, then **drawdown**. On a standing investment loan this is often a single drawing. On a development loan it is staged. Commercial rent in the UK is commonly due on the quarter days (25 March, 24 June, 29 September, 25 December); EM Law notes that interest payment dates are often set a couple of weeks later, to give time to chase late tenants. Rent frequently lands in a lender-controlled account and is applied down a "waterfall" (interest first, then other agreed items, with surplus only then released to the borrower).

## 3. The rules in the loan, in plain English

A **covenant** is a promise. In this context it is a promise in the loan contract, not "the tenant's covenant" (which is a separate phrase meaning how reliable a tenant is at paying rent).

Hamlins (October 2023) and EM Law both describe financial covenants as an early-warning system: a breach does not automatically mean the borrower is insolvent. The levels are set so the lender can step in before recovery of the loan is at risk.

### 3.1 Financial covenants

**Loan-to-value (LTV)**  
Loan ÷ current market value of the property. The borrower must keep this below an agreed percentage. It is tested off a valuation by an independent valuer acceptable to the lender. The agreement usually says how often the lender can call a new valuation, typically at the borrower's cost (Hamlins). If values fall, LTV rises even if the landlord has paid every instalment.

The LMA real estate finance document, as described in a Jones Day white paper on the LMA REF form, includes a maintenance LTV covenant: the company must ensure LTV does not at any time exceed an agreed percentage. (The LMA has updated the REF documents since that 2012 paper, including a financial-covenant cure rider and optional cash-trap language — LexisNexis.)

**Interest cover ratio (ICR)**  
Net rental income ÷ interest (and often lender fees) for the same period, usually a quarter or a rolling twelve months (Hamlins).

"Net" matters. Hamlins defines net rental income as rent after deductions such as ground rent, property maintenance costs and taxes. Service charge collected from tenants to run the building is generally not treated as the landlord's income available to pay interest (Arthur Cox, 2021). For BTR and student housing, lenders typically start from **net operating income** — rent after voids, unpaid rent and operating costs — not from headline gross rent (Build to Rent Finance; Student Accommodation Finance).

ICR can be:

- **Historic** (look-back): actual income received vs actual interest and fees. Arrears immediately hurt this number.
- **Projected** (look-forward): estimated future income vs estimated interest, using conservative assumptions. Hamlins says those assumptions usually include a worst case: a tenant exercises a break at the earliest date, a tenant already in arrears is assumed not to pay future rent, and vacant space is not assumed to be re-let immediately.

The LMA form includes both Historical Interest Cover and Projected Interest Cover as ongoing maintenance covenants (Jones Day on the LMA REF document).

**Debt service cover / debt cover (DSCR)**  
Used when the loan is amortising (being paid down). Net income ÷ (interest + fees + principal) (Hamlins). BTR investment debt is often described as being sized off DSCR as well as ICR (Build to Rent Finance, indicative 1.3–1.5x). This note does **not** treat that broker range as a market-wide covenant standard.

**Debt yield**  
Property net income ÷ loan amount. It does not move just because the valuation moves, so it is a check against a loan that looks fine on LTV only because the price is high. Bayes' YE 2023 media report defined it this way and said that, historically, debt yields of **4–7%** indicated higher leverage and higher risk, and **8–12%** indicated lower leverage and lower risk. That is a 2023 historical range, not a 2025 typical covenant level. This research did not find a reliable 2025 market-wide "typical debt-yield covenant".

**Occupancy or minimum rent.** On operating residential assets (BTR, student housing), lenders underwrite occupancy and the stability of collections, not just a single lease (Student Accommodation Finance; Build to Rent Finance). On commercial property, a projected ICR test already punishes vacancy and tenant breaks (Hamlins). This research did not find a reliable market-wide minimum occupancy percentage written as a standard covenant (Arthur Cox notes that "stabilisation" assumptions in development valuations are often at least 80% occupancy, but that is a valuation assumption, not a stated standard ongoing covenant).

**WALT (weighted average lease term) and tenant concentration.** WALT is the average unexpired length of the leases, weighted by rent. Lenders care because a loan is harder to refinance if the leases expire in the same year as the loan. Insurers in particular are described as preferring long, secure lease profiles (Barrow Street). **Tenant concentration** is the risk that one tenant provides too much of the rent: if that tenant leaves or fails, income collapses. This research did **not** find a reliable UK market-wide typical covenant number for a minimum WALT or a maximum tenant-concentration percentage, so none is stated here.

**Cash trap / cash sweep vs default.** A cash trap is an *earlier* warning than the default covenant (Arthur Cox, 2021; confirmed as an optional LMA rider by LexisNexis). If LTV or cover reaches a tighter "watch" level, surplus cash that would have been paid out to the borrower is instead held in a blocked account. Lenders may then require that cash to prepay the loan, or may hold it until the test has been met for a recovery period — Arthur Cox says a commonly agreed release test is compliance for **two consecutive interest periods**. A cash sweep is a closely related idea: surplus cash is applied to repay the loan rather than released. This research did **not** find a reliable market-wide pair of numbers such as "cash trap at X% LTV / default at Y% LTV", so no typical percentages are given. The important design point for Canary is that there are usually *two* levels: a tighter early-warning level, and a looser default level.

**Equity cure.** The owner injects cash, as equity or a subordinated loan, to fix a financial-covenant breach (Arthur Cox; Hamlins; LMA financial-covenant cure rider as reported by LexisNexis). The common real-estate position is that the cash is treated as *reducing the loan* for the test, which is tougher on the borrower than treating it as extra income. Use is limited: there is usually a cap on how many times it can be used over the life of the loan, and on how often it can be used in consecutive periods, so the borrower cannot permanently mask a deteriorating building. If the cash is deposited rather than used to prepay, LexisNexis reports that the LMA rider allows withdrawal after two interest periods of compliance, and otherwise lets majority lenders apply it to prepay the loan.

### 3.2 Information covenants

The borrower must keep the lender informed. Count Ready (guide updated August 2026, sources last reviewed 1 August 2026) and Hamlins describe typical packages:

- Annual accounts and, often, quarterly management figures.
- A **rent roll** (who occupies what, at what rent, until when, with breaks and incentives).
- Arrears, voids, service-charge information, and notice of a tenant problem.
- Insurance evidence.
- Valuations and inspection access.
- A **compliance certificate**, usually signed by the borrower, confirming that the financial tests are met, delivered with the figures and property reports (Hamlins).
- Prompt notice of material events (litigation, damage, a breach, a change of control).

Late or missing information can itself be a breach, even if the ratios would have passed (Count Ready).

### 3.3 Asset / property covenants and statutory compliance

These promises keep the security safe and lettable:

- **Insurance** — keep proper buildings (and often loss-of-rent) insurance in force, with the lender's interest noted (Count Ready; EM Law).
- **Repair and maintenance** — keep the property in good condition (Count Ready; EM Law).
- **Comply with law** — planning, licensing, fire and building safety, environmental duties. Pinsent Masons (3 November 2025) reports that the LMA view is that generic "comply with all laws if failure would have a material adverse effect" wording may already cover the Building Safety Act 2022, but many lenders find that too weak (because "material adverse effect" is negotiated) and instead want specific Building Safety Act or fire-safety wording, plus positive information undertakings. Lenders and buyers are frequently asking for updated **EWS1** forms, PAS 9980 assessments, a Type 4 fire-risk assessment on higher-risk buildings, and the "golden thread" of safety information. Known, unremediated defects make financing and full-value sales difficult.
- **Fire risk assessments (FRAs), electrical reports (EICRs) and asbestos surveys** are treated by DMH Stallard (29 May 2026) as non-negotiable due-diligence items, not formalities. An FRA is required under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 for virtually all non-domestic premises and for the common parts of residential buildings, and must be kept under review. In England, private landlords must obtain a satisfactory EICR at least every five years (Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020; also GOV.UK guidance). Missing, out-of-date or unsatisfactory reports can prejudice insurance and the value of the lender's security, and can make the building hard to sell or re-let on enforcement.
- **Gas safety.** GOV.UK's private-renting guidance: gas appliances and flues must be checked annually by a Gas Safe engineer, and the record given to the tenant.
- **HMO licensing.** GOV.UK: a mandatory licence is required for a house in multiple occupation rented to five or more people from more than one household who share facilities. Lending criteria often treat licensed HMOs as a distinct product; PRA SS13/16 also lists licence fees among the costs that should feed the ICR.
- **LOLER** (the Lifting Operations and Lifting Equipment Regulations, which cover lift safety) was not found named in a facility-agreement or law-firm loan-covenant source during this research. It is still a statutory duty where lifts are in use, so it belongs in the same "keep the building legally compliant and insurable" bucket as FRA and EICR, but this note does not claim that loan contracts typically use the word LOLER.

### 3.4 Restrictions that need lender consent

Count Ready and EM Law: the borrower usually needs written lender consent before granting or materially changing a lease, selling or transferring the property, taking more debt or a second charge, changing use or doing major works, or changing ownership or control of the borrower. Planning permission or a landlord's consent does not replace the mortgage lender's consent. Proceeding without it can itself be a default.

### 3.5 Events of default

Typical events (Hamlins; Jones Day on the LMA REF form; Count Ready) include: a financial-covenant breach; non-payment; a breach of other undertakings after any agreed grace period; misrepresentation; insolvency; security problems; and sometimes a "material adverse change" clause. A default usually lets the lender demand immediate repayment (**acceleration**) and enforce its security — subject to any cure rights, waivers or grace periods in the contract.

### 3.6 How residential, BTR/student and commercial loans differ

| | Buy-to-let mortgage | BTR / student block loan | Commercial office / retail / industrial loan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who is the borrower? | An individual, or a small SPV company | Usually a company / SPV | Usually a company / SPV |
| What pays the loan? | Rent from one home or a small portfolio | Occupancy, collections and operating costs across many units | The business lease(s) and the tenant's strength |
| Extra backstop | Often the landlord's personal income and, for company loans, a personal guarantee (e.g. The Mortgage Works requires director guarantees) | The block's net operating income; operator quality on student housing | The lease contract, unexpired term, and tenant covenant |
| Main tests at the start | PRA-style ICR (industry standard 125%, often 145% for higher-rate taxpayers) at a stressed rate; common high-street max LTV 75% | Sized on NOI vs DSCR/ICR and LTV; Bayes 2025 average origination LTV 58.6% residential / 57.3% student, ICR 1.2x / 1.4x | Bayes 2025 average origination LTV mid-to-high 50s and ICR 1.3x–1.8x by sector |
| Ongoing watchpoints | Rent received, arrears, costs, refinancing at the end of a fix, portfolio-level ICR for 4+ properties | Occupancy, arrears, operating-cost creep, safety compliance on a residential block | Tenant failure or expiry, vacancy, lease breaks, value falls, safety/insurance |
| Rulebook | PRA SS13/16 for BTL mortgages | Specialist CRE / investment or development facility, often LMA-style on larger deals | Same as left |

## 4. What happens if a rule is broken

In rough order of seriousness, from the sources above:

1. **Talk and information.** Confirm the actual contract definition, gather evidence, and give any notice the contract requires (Count Ready). Early, accurate notice usually preserves more options.
2. **Waiver.** The lender can agree to let a particular test or event pass, often for one date only, sometimes with a fee, tighter reporting or conditions. A waiver is not the same as deleting the covenant (Count Ready).
3. **Cure.** Within an agreed period the borrower prepays part of the loan, lodges cash as extra security, or injects equity (Hamlins; Arthur Cox; LMA cure rider).
4. **Cash trap or cash sweep.** Surplus rent is locked or used to pay down the loan, so equity gets nothing until the tests recover (Arthur Cox).
5. **Default and acceleration.** If it is not fixed, the lender can call the whole loan in and enforce security (Hamlins).
6. **Enforcement / LPA receivership.** Under the Law of Property Act 1925, and usually under enhanced powers in the mortgage deed, the lender can appoint a receiver of the property (UK Finance policy briefing, 14 March 2018, still published in the UK Finance document library as of the June 2025 file date; HM Land Registry Practice Guide 36A). The court is not involved in the appointment. The receiver acts as agent of the *borrower*, collects rent, manages the property, and — if the mortgage allows — can sell it and use the proceeds to repay the loan. UK Finance says appointment may be considered not only for missed payments, but also for an **LTV covenant breach**, local-authority enforcement, unpaid ground rent or service charge, or poor maintenance. For BTL, a receiver is often used precisely because the lender does not want to step into the landlord's shoes (gas safety, licensing, service charges) while tenants are in occupation.

## 5. Worked examples (ILLUSTRATIVE numbers)

These sums exist only to show the arithmetic. They are not a real building, a real loan, or a market average dressed up as a case study. The starting LTVs are *in the same neighbourhood* as Bayes 2025 origination averages, and the BTR cost ratio is *in the same neighbourhood* as the broker range of 25–35% of gross rent, but the pounds themselves are made up.

### 5.1 ILLUSTRATIVE: a residential build-to-rent block

**Starting position**

- Agreed value of the block: **£20,000,000**
- Loan, interest-only: **£12,000,000**
- **LTV** = 12,000,000 ÷ 20,000,000 = **60%**
- Illustrative interest rate: **6%** a year
- Annual interest = 12,000,000 × 0.06 = **£720,000**
- Gross rent if every flat is let and paying: **£1,600,000** a year
- Voids, unpaid rent and operating costs: **30%** of gross rent = **£480,000**
- Net operating income (NOI) = 1,600,000 − 480,000 = **£1,120,000**
- **ICR** = 1,120,000 ÷ 720,000 = **1.56x** (also 156%)

Suppose the facility says: LTV must stay at or below **65%**, and historic ICR must stay at or above **1.30x**. (Those thresholds are illustrative too.) The loan is comfortably inside both tests.

**What goes wrong**

Over a quarter, arrears rise and a service-charge budget overspend has to be funded by the landlord, so:

- Collected gross rent falls to **£1,350,000** (some tenants have not paid).
- Operating costs rise to **£450,000**.
- NOI = 1,350,000 − 450,000 = **£900,000**
- **ICR** = 900,000 ÷ 720,000 = **1.25x**

1.25 is below the illustrative 1.30 test, so the income covenant is breached — even if the borrower has still managed to pay this quarter's interest out of reserves. That is exactly the gap Canary is meant to flag early: the rule has already broken, or is about to, before the quarterly certificate is signed.

If a fire-risk assessment or EWS1 is also overdue, that may not change the ICR arithmetic on day one, but it can:

- breach the statutory-compliance / "comply with laws" and insurance covenants (DMH Stallard; Pinsent Masons);
- make a valuer haircut the value, which lifts LTV;
- block a refinance at loan maturity, because the next lender will ask for the same documents.

If a valuer cut the value from £20 million to £17 million because of safety uncertainty and weaker income, LTV would become 12,000,000 ÷ 17,000,000 = **70.6%**, which would also break the illustrative 65% LTV cap.

### 5.2 ILLUSTRATIVE: a commercial office loan

**Starting position**

- Agreed value: **£15,000,000**
- Loan, interest-only: **£8,500,000**
- **LTV** = 8,500,000 ÷ 15,000,000 = **56.7%**
- Illustrative interest rate: **6%**
- Annual interest = 8,500,000 × 0.06 = **£510,000**
- Net rental income (after ground rent and non-recoverable costs): **£816,000**
- **ICR** = 816,000 ÷ 510,000 = **1.60x**

Illustrative covenants: LTV no more than **65%**; historic and projected ICR at least **1.30x**.

**What goes wrong**

A major tenant leaves, or is already in arrears so the projected-ICR assumptions exclude their future rent (the Hamlins projected-test conservative assumptions). Remaining net rent is **£500,000**.

- **Projected ICR** = 500,000 ÷ 510,000 = **0.98x** — a clear breach of 1.30x.
- The historic test will follow once the empty period appears in actual collections.
- A valuer, capitalising the lower income, might cut the value to £11,000,000. **LTV** = 8,500,000 ÷ 11,000,000 = **77.3%**, which also breaks the 65% cap.

The landlord can try to cure by paying down the loan or lodging cash, ask for a waiver while a new tenant is found, or face a cash trap and, if nothing is agreed, default and a possible receiver.

## 6. How Cortile signals map onto these covenants

Cortile is a block-management dataset (leases, service charges, compliance, tickets). It does not replace a valuation or a loan agreement, but it is early evidence of the things those agreements test.

| Cortile signal | Covenant or rule it threatens | Why, in plain English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lease **arrears** | Historic ICR / DSCR; projected ICR (tenants already in arrears are often assumed not to pay future rent); occupancy / collections on BTR and student loans; information covenants (rent roll and arrears reporting) | The loan is paid from rent received, not rent invoiced. Unpaid rent shrinks net income and can flip a projected test immediately. |
| **Service-charge budget vs actual overspend** | ICR / DSCR (if the landlord has to fund the shortfall or if non-recoverable costs rise); cash / liquidity; in serious cases, the same strain UK Finance lists as a reason to consider a receiver (unpaid service charge) | Service charge collected from tenants is usually not "income" for interest cover, but an overspend the landlord cannot recover is an extra cost. On residential blocks, rising operating costs are a direct hit to NOI. |
| Overdue **FRA** (fire risk assessment) | Statutory / "comply with laws" and insurance covenants; information covenants; LTV via valuation and refinance risk | FRAs are a legal duty for common parts and non-domestic premises. Lenders treat missing or stale FRAs as a security and insurance problem (DMH Stallard; Pinsent Masons). |
| Overdue **EICR** (electrical installation condition report) | Same as FRA; also a specific residential-landlord legal duty in England (at least every five years) | An unsatisfactory or missing EICR is a regulatory, insurance and lettability problem, and therefore a security-value problem. |
| Overdue **EWS1** (or equivalent fire-safety / PAS 9980 evidence) on a relevant residential building | Same as FRA, plus practical refinance and valuation risk | Pinsent Masons report that lenders frequently ask for an updated EWS1 (and related assessments) on a financing. Without it, a deal delays or the value is chipped. |
| Overdue **LOLER** (lift safety) | Statutory compliance / repair / insurance bucket | Not found named in the loan-agreement sources reviewed. Still a statutory lift-safety duty, so treat as legal-compliance and repair risk, and be honest that the contract may not use this label. |
| **Emergency and repeat tickets** | Repair covenant; ICR / DSCR via higher operating costs and possible voids; insurance; in extremes, value / LTV and occupancy (uninhabitable units or closed plant) | A stream of emergency repairs is evidence the building is not being kept in the condition the loan requires, and it is cash leaving the net-income figure. |

For Canary, the clean mapping is: **arrears and cost overruns threaten the income tests (ICR/DSCR) first**; **overdue safety compliance threatens the asset, insurance, value (LTV) and "comply with laws" tests**, and can also make the next refinance fail even if interest is still being paid.

## 7. What this research could not verify

- A market-wide "typical" cash-trap level versus default level (for example a standard LTV or ICR pair). The mechanic is well sourced; the numbers are deal-specific.
- A market-wide typical minimum WALT or maximum tenant-concentration covenant.
- A 2025 typical debt-yield *covenant* level (only a 2023 historical risk range from Bayes).
- A standard ongoing occupancy-floor covenant (as opposed to underwriting appetite and valuation assumptions).
- Facility-agreement wording that names LOLER specifically.
- A single maximum LTV that applies to every BTL lender (75% is a common high-street cap; some specialist products go to 80%).
- That every limited-company BTL lender requires a personal guarantee (The Mortgage Works does, in its published criteria; others may differ).
